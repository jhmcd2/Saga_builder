There could definitely be a story there. Dreams often compress unrelated ideas into a single narrative, and yours has several strong story elements that fit together surprisingly well.

Some of the pieces you described are familiar science fiction motifs, but the combination feels fairly distinctive:

A rocket launch and engine failure, with you trying to understand the engineering, sounds like your mind borrowing from Apollo 13 or perhaps other Saturn V launch stories. Dreams often incorporate something you've recently thought about without making it the main plot.
A vast desert city that feels like Stargate Atlantis, but with a more stylized, almost anime or The Transformers aesthetic.
A blue-haired woman who seems important without you initially knowing why.
The biggest twist: you think you've been away for a short expedition, but when you return, the city has become an archaeological site buried beneath millennia of sand.
The discovery that the woman's skeleton belongs to someone you knew "earlier," even though she actually lived long after you left. From your perspective, she died 10,000 years ago; from hers, you disappeared long before her lifetime ended.

That last idea is particularly interesting because it isn't the usual time-travel paradox.

Normally stories go:

Meet someone → leave → return to the future.

Yours goes:

Meet someone in what you think is the present → leave briefly → unknowingly jump forward by ten thousand years → discover the civilization's remains → realize that from your perspective the person you knew has been dead for ten millennia.

The emotional impact isn't that you failed to save her. It's that time itself stole the relationship.

The line you remembered—

"the only woman that ever loved me died ten thousand years ago"

—is melodramatic in the way dreams often are, but it's also a genuinely compelling sci-fi premise. It reframes archaeology as personal grief.

A few story ideas naturally grow from it:

The "short trip" was actually passing through a natural temporal anomaly. Nobody notices because subjective time barely changes.
The city wasn't abandoned—it simply continued for centuries after you vanished. The woman spent years believing you'd return.
The bones are identified because she's buried with something you gave her, or she's posed in a way that indicates she expected you to come back.
Everyone else sees an archaeological dig. Only you recognize the person beneath the sand.

The blue hair also works symbolically. In dreams, vivid physical features often exist simply so someone remains recognizable after a long time skip. It also reinforces the slightly mythic, almost animated feel you described.

The most striking image, to me, is this sequence:

You walk through a gleaming desert metropolis.

You leave for what feels like an afternoon.

You return to silence.

Half the towers are dunes.

You're no longer an explorer—you've become an archaeologist excavating your own memories.

Then you uncover the skeleton of someone whose face you still remember.

That's a powerful sci-fi image because it combines cosmic time with an intimate loss. It feels like something that could sit alongside works such as Interstellar or The Time Machine, where the emotional consequences of time are more important than the mechanics.

Dreams don't necessarily have hidden meanings, but if you wanted to turn this into fiction, the emotional core seems clear: the tragedy isn't death—it's returning too late to a place you thought you'd never really left.

I actually think that version is stronger than the first one, because it changes the story from "time travel accident" into "one person understood the truth before anyone else."

The chronology would look something like this:

You arrive in what appears to be an impossibly advanced city.
Neither you nor your companions realize you're actually 10,000+ years in the past.
Most people there don't know either—or perhaps they simply don't care where you came from.
The blue-haired woman gradually realizes something is wrong. Maybe your language, stars, biology, or records don't make sense.
She discovers that your appearance is a closed causal loop: history already records that your group vanished. There is no future in which you stay.
She quietly accepts that the only way to preserve history—and save you—is to send you back through whatever temporal mechanism brought you there.
You think you're returning home after a brief excursion.
Instead, you return to your own era.
The city is now buried.
You excavate it and discover she lived another thousand years after you left...waiting, building, or simply growing old.
You find her remains and only then realize what she sacrificed.

That gives the story a tragic asymmetry.

For you, it was an afternoon.

For her, it was an entire lifetime.

And she knew it.

What I especially like is the implication that she never expected to see you again. She's not sacrificing herself in the sense of dying immediately. She's sacrificing the rest of her life.

Imagine if she says something that seems innocuous before you leave:

"You'll remember me."

At the time it sounds flirtatious.

Ten thousand years later, standing over her grave, you realize she wasn't asking a question or making a hope. She was making a prediction.

Or perhaps she says:

"I have more time than you."

You interpret it as meaning she's immortal or long-lived.

Later you realize she meant she literally had another thousand years to live after you disappeared.

There's also an elegant thematic twist available.

You initially assume the civilization was destroyed by some catastrophe.

Instead, as you uncover the archaeological record, you learn it declined naturally over thousands of years. The greatest tragedy wasn't the fall of the city—it was that one woman spent centuries carrying the memory of people who vanished from history.

Maybe there are murals depicting your group.

Maybe legends describe "the visitors who returned to the heavens."

Maybe every historian dismissed those stories as myth.

Then you uncover a personal tomb. Alongside her remains is an object that only you would recognize—a tool, a keepsake, or a handwritten note in your own language. That's the first undeniable proof that the myths were true.

The emotional climax isn't "we traveled through time." The audience probably figures that out earlier. The climax is when you finally understand that she knew all along, smiled anyway, and chose to let you go because there was no version of history where both of you could stay together.

That ending has the kind of bittersweet inevitability that tends to linger with readers: not because anyone made a mistake, but because everyone made the only choice they could.
