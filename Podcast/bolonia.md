But because you're describing a leader whose public justification is not necessarily their actual motivation.

A leader can say:

"I'm protecting my people."

while pursuing something profoundly self-serving.

And importantly, propaganda doesn't need to be completely false.

It can contain enough truth to make people accept the lie.

Your Queen could genuinely believe:

"Our civilization is dying."

That's true.

She could genuinely believe:

"Earth contains something that could save us."

Also true.

She could genuinely believe:

"Humans are inferior."

Also true within her ideology.

And then she could say:

"Therefore we must save humanity."

Which is the questionable part.

She has simply substituted:

"save humanity"

for

"extract what we need from humanity."

That is a much more believable political lie.

And Her Civilization May Not Even Know the Truth
This is another thing I'd preserve.

Don't necessarily make the Queen the only person who knows what's happening.

Her own government could be operating under competing narratives.

Scientists may think they're conducting reproductive research.

Military officials may think they're preparing for possible human aggression.

Religious authorities may think Earth is an abomination.

The public might believe humanity is being studied for eventual diplomatic contact.

Meanwhile, the Queen has a completely different agenda.

That allows you to have characters who sincerely believe the official story.

Which makes the Queen much harder to read.

The Most Important Question: Why Destroy Earth?
And I think the answer should not be:

"Because humanity was going to destroy itself."

That would weaken your premise.

Instead, make the question deliberately unanswered for a long time.

The protagonist eventually asks:

"If you came here to save us, why did you try to destroy us?"

And the Queen gives him an answer.

Maybe it's incredibly convincing.

Maybe she says:

"Because you were already beyond saving."

Or:

"Because what I needed could no longer be obtained peacefully."

Or:

"Because your species became a threat."

Or:

"Because the experiment failed."

Or:

"Because there was no other choice."

And the protagonist realizes:

Every answer is something she could have said regardless of the truth.

That's the Bugonia influence you're describing.

The audience isn't supposed to know whether she's telling the truth.

But Your Backstory Gives the Audience Something the Original Doesn't
And this is the part I think you're really accomplishing.

The original story can leave:

"Why are they doing this?"

as an unanswered mystery.

Your story can provide a credible historical mechanism that makes their presence on Earth understandable:

Their civilization has a reproductive crisis.

But you don't have to provide a definitive answer to:

"What does the Queen ultimately intend to do about it?"

That's where the mystery remains.

And the boy makes it exponentially worse.

Because now the Queen has discovered something she couldn't have planned for:

Her own son is on Earth.

A male.

A product of the biological phenomenon her civilization has spent thousands of years eliminating.

Possibly the most powerful psionic since the Event.

And potentially capable of changing the fate of an entire planet.

So when she says:

"I came here to save humanity."

the audience has a reason to ask:

"Did you?"

And perhaps the most frightening answer is:

She doesn't even know anymore.

She may have started with one objective, discovered another, become obsessed with her son, and gradually constructed justifications for whatever she needed to do next.

That would make her much more interesting than a straightforward villain—and much closer to the unsettling ambiguity you're looking for.
