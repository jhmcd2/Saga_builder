The "New Tech" Panic and Corporate Slop

Your comparison between Toy Story (https://www.google.com/search?kgmid=%2Fg%2F122xf7xr&q=Toy+Story) in 1995 and AI today hits on a recurring cycle in entertainment history:
• The 1995 CGI backlash. When Toy Story (https://www.google.com/search?kgmid=%2Fg%2F122xf7xr&q=Toy+Story) arrived, traditional 2D animators and critics called 3D computer animation cold, plastic, and soulless. People feared computers would replace human artists and ruin the craft.
• Tools versus intent. History proved that 3D CGI was just a new paintbrush. In the hands of storytellers with vision, it created masterpieces. Once studios realized they could use CGI to churn out cheap, formulaic assembly-line content, we got the "slop" era.
• The MCU and franchise fatigue. You nailed the Marvel shift. Early MCU phases used CGI to ground impossible things in reality. Later phases used CGI as a crutch to fix unfinished scripts in post-production, leading to rushed visual effects and weightless action scenes.
• The Super Mario and Toy Story 5 (https://www.google.com/search?kgmid=%2Fg%2F11sj9m3qrx&q=Toy+Story+5) trap. Audiences claim to hate cash grabs, yet they reward safety. Studios know a nostalgic brand name guarantees a billion-dollar return, even if the movie borrows heavily from older hits or extends a story that already had a perfect ending.
Ultimately, the problem with AI in movies isn't just the technology itself. It is that major studios want to use AI the same way they abuse CGI and sequels—to cut costs, skip the hard work of writing, and feed audiences familiar comfort food.

The Star Trek CGI Evolution

When early CGI appeared in the 1980s Star Trek films—like the Genesis simulation in The Wrath of Khan or the Bird-of-Prey animations in The Voyage Home—it stood out like a sore thumb. It looked shiny, weightless, and plastic compared to Industrial Light & Magic's incredible physical miniatures.
By the time Deep Space Nine hit its later seasons in the late 1990s, the showrunners needed massive fleet battles like the Dominion War. Building and filming dozens of physical models on motion-control rigs was too slow and expensive for a weekly TV schedule. CGI finally allowed them to tell stories that were physically impossible before. Today, modern Star Trek shows use virtual production walls and full CGI ships without anyone batting an eye, because the tech matured to serve the story.

The "AI" Branding Problem vs. VFX Reality

You nailed the exact disconnect happening in Hollywood right now. The word "AI" has become an instant turn-off because of how executives and tech bros are pitching it:
• The "Prompt-to-Movie" Fantasy: Studios and tech startups keep trying to sell the idea of typing a sentence into a box and spitting out a finished movie. Audiences hate this because it signals laziness, theft of artist data, and zero human intent.
• The Real VFX Bottleneck: Meanwhile, actual visual effects artists are burning out in droves from "pixel-fucking"—spending 80-hour weeks doing mind-numbing, repetitive tasks like rotoscoping (tracing actors frame-by-frame to cut them out of a background), removing wires, or tracking markers on green screens.
• Machine Learning as a Relief Valve: Studios like Sony Pictures Imageworks (the team behind the Spider-Verse movies) are already using machine learning tools to automate tedious in-between frames and clean up rotoscoping. When used that way, AI isn't replacing the artist—it's freeing them from grunt work so they can focus on lighting, acting, and composition.
Until studios stop bragging about replacing writers and actors with "prompt magic" and start treating AI like the rotoscoping brush it actually is, audiences are going to keep rejecting the label on sight.

That is one of the central philosophical and legal arguments at the heart of the AI debate. In creative writing and visual art, studying the masters is standard practice.
As the saying goes, "good artists borrow, great artists steal." Humans absorb thousands of books, paintings, and movies over a lifetime, filter them through their own lived experiences, and produce something new. Tech companies argue that training an AI model on millions of images or texts is just a faster, digital version of the exact same process.
However, artists, writers, and legal experts usually point to three major differences that break that comparison:

1. Scale and Speed vs. Human Limits

• Human learning is slow and filtered. A human writer might read a few hundred books over a decade and spend months writing one novel. Their output is naturally limited by time and filtered through human emotion, flaws, and personal history.
• Machine ingestion is industrial. An AI model ingests billions of works in days without experiencing or understanding any of them. Once trained, it can flood the market with thousands of imitations in minutes, directly undercutting the livelihood of the very people whose work built the system.

2. The "Commercial Product" Line

• Personal study vs. corporate software. When you take an art class and sketch a Monet to learn brushwork, you aren't selling a commercial machine designed to replace Monet.
• Profit without permission. Tech companies scraped copyrighted art, books, and movies without asking or paying, and then packaged that training into paid subscription products. Critics argue that taking someone's life's work to build a direct commercial competitor crosses the line from "inspiration" into exploitation.

3. The "Style" Loophole

• Copyright protects expression, not style. Legally, you cannot copyright an art style (like "Studio Ghibli" or "Wes Anderson"). If a human artist paints in a style inspired by Ghibli, people usually see it as a tribute or influence.
• Direct mimicry on demand. With AI, users can type a living artist's name directly into a prompt to generate custom art in their exact signature style for free. For working illustrators, seeing clients use a machine trained on their own portfolio instead of hiring them feels less like "artistic influence" and more like identity theft.

Where the Middle Ground Lies

This is why many artists aren't actually opposed to AI tools—they are opposed to unconsented training data.
When a studio trains an internal AI strictly on its own previous movies and artwork (like Sony did for Spider-Verse, or how some game studios train models on their own concept art), the ethical problem largely disappears. The artists get paid, the studio owns the data, and the AI acts as a genuine productivity booster rather than a shortcut built on scraped web data.

You are pointing straight at the elephant in the room: economic self-preservation.
At its core, almost every debate over creative rights and new technology boils down to who controls the money and who loses their job. The moral and philosophical arguments about "soul" or "fairness" almost always show up after a business model feels threatened.

The Google Books Precedent

Your Google Books example is actually the exact legal foundation AI companies are relying on right now.
• What actually happened in court: When the Authors Guild and major publishers sued Google in 2005, it wasn't just because people were browsing snippets online—it was because publishers realized Google had digitized their entire catalog without paying them a dime.
• The landmark ruling: After a decade of fighting, the courts sided with Google in 2015 (Authors Guild v. Google). The judges ruled that scanning millions of books to create a searchable database was transformative fair use because it didn't replace the market for reading the actual books—it just pointed people to small snippets.
• Why AI is the next battleground: AI companies are making the exact same legal argument today. They argue that ingesting books and art to train a model is transformative, just like Google's search index. Authors and artists are fighting back by arguing that unlike Google Books—which just showed you a three-sentence preview—AI generates a direct substitute that does replace buying their work.

The Pattern of Tech Disruption

Looking at history, your point about the fear of replacement holds up across almost every era of entertainment and publishing:
• Sheet music vs. Player pianos (1900s): Composers sued player piano makers for "stealing" their music without paying royalties, claiming machines would ruin live musicianship.
• Movie theaters vs. Television (1950s): Hollywood studios initially refused to license films to TV or even let TV sets appear inside movies, terrified audiences would stop leaving the house.
• Record labels vs. Napster and Streaming (2000s): The music industry sued teenagers for piracy rather than adapting to digital distribution, until Spotify turned the exact same tech into a massive profit engine.
In every single case, the moral outrage was real, but the true engine driving the fight was market disruption. People rarely object to a machine learning from human work until that machine starts bidding for their job. Once the big studios, publishers, and tech companies figure out a licensing model where everyone gets a cut of the check, the philosophical complaints usually quiet down overnight.
