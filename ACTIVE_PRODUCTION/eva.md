To look at the real-world geology and archaeology of Japan, we have to separate when the actual landmass formed from when humans first arrived to live on it.
Here is how the timeline breaks down:

The Geological Age (The Land)

The physical islands of Japan are relatively young in Earth's history, having formed roughly 15 million to 30 million years ago.
• The Breakup: Originally, the land that is now Japan was attached to the eastern coast of the Eurasian continent (near present-day Russia, China, and the Koreas).
• The Opening of the Sea of Japan: Around 20 to 15 million years ago, intense tectonic activity caused the earth's crust to rip apart, pulling the land outward into the Pacific Ocean. The gap left behind filled with water, creating the Sea of Japan.
• The Volcanic Arc: Japan is positioned right on the "Pacific Ring of Fire," where four tectonic plates crash together. This collision forced land upward and triggered massive volcanic activity, creating the mountainous islands we see today.

The Archaeological Age (The People)

If you are looking at when Japan became an active site for human archaeology, that began roughly 35,000 to 40,000 years ago.
• The Ice Age Land Bridges: During the last Ice Age, sea levels were significantly lower than they are today. Glaciers locked up so much water that shallow seas dried out, creating physical land bridges that connected Japan back to mainland Asia (in both the north via Sakhalin and the south via the Korean Peninsula).
• The First Paleolithic Settlers: Prehistoric humans simply walked across these land bridges, following migrating game like mammoths and giant elephants. Archaeologists have found thousands of Paleolithic sites across Japan containing stone tools, knives, and butchered animal bones dating back to this 35,000-year mark.
• Becoming an Island Nation: As the Ice Age ended around 10,000 to 15,000 years ago, the glaciers melted, sea levels rose, and the land bridges vanished underwater. The people trapped on the land became isolated, adapting to the new environment and marking the beginning of the Jōmon Period, famous for creating some of the oldest pottery in human history.

The "Divine Hands" Fabrication Crisis

Starting in the late 1970s and 1980s, a self-taught amateur archaeologist named Shinichi Fujimura began "discovering" older and older stone tools in northern Japan.
• The Claim: His finds pushed the timeline of humans in Japan back from 30,000 years to 40,000 years, then 300,000 years, and eventually to an astonishing 600,000 years ago.
• The "Need to Believe": This tapped heavily into a sense of national pride. The media ran front-page headlines, and school textbooks were completely rewritten to boast that Japan possessed some of the oldest human habitations on Earth. Because Fujimura had an uncanny knack for finding exactly what people wanted to see, he was nicknamed "Divine Hands."
• The Mainstream Blindspot: Mainstream Japanese archaeologists were so eager to validate this narrative of a deep, ancient Japanese ancestry that they fiercely defended his work and ignored skeptics for two decades.

The Crash: November 2000

The entire illusion shattered on November 5, 2000. Journalists from the Mainichi Shimbun newspaper grew suspicious of his unbelievable luck and set up hidden cameras at his excavation site. They caught Fujimura on video sneaking onto the site in the early morning, digging holes, and burying stone tools from his own private collection before "discovering" them later with his team.
Confronted with the photographic evidence, Fujimura collapsed into tears at a press conference, admitting he had fabricated everything.

The Aftermath

The Japanese Archaeological Association launched a massive multi-year investigation and concluded that Fujimura had faked discoveries at 162 different Paleolithic sites over 20 years.
The academic community was deeply humiliated. Textbooks had to be recalled and entirely rewritten yet again. Since that massive reality check in 2000, Japan's archaeological community has rigorously reset its timeline, firmly anchoring the arrival of humans at that ~38,000-year mark via migrations from the Asian mainland.

The contrast you are pointing out between Western views on the "cradle of humanity" and Japan’s stubborn cultural narratives touches on a fascinating intersection of psychology, geography, and how modern nationalism works. You are entirely right that on a scientific, academic level, Japan is a highly secular, scientifically advanced nation that does teach the African origin of humanity in its schools.
Yet, the cultural impulse to treat Japan as the "center of the world" or an "isolated cradle" persists in media, tropes, and that 1980s archaeological scandal. There are a few core reasons why this narrative is so potent there compared to the West.

1. The Island Nation Complex (Shimaguni Konjaku)

Europeans and Americans have historically been expansionist, imperial cultures. Western narratives are often about going out into the world, conquering it, or restructuring it (which is why Western media loves space exploration or global intervention tropes). The West acknowledges the Middle East or Africa as the biological/historical starting point because Western history is inherently a story of migration, movement, and global mixing.
Japan, by contrast, has a deep-seated cultural identity built on geographical isolation.
• For centuries, Japan was physically and politically cut off from the rest of the world (especially during the 250-year Sakoku isolation period).
• This created a psychological worldview where the world is divided into two categories: Japan and The Outside.
• In pop culture and sci-fi, when a global threat happens, it feels natural to a Japanese audience that the narrative centers entirely on their "island fortress," because the cultural lens has always viewed Japan as a distinct, self-contained universe.

2. State Shinto's Lasting Cultural Echo

You perfectly noted that Japan lacks Abrahamic religious history (Adam, Eve, the Garden of Eden in the Middle East). But they do have their own creation myth that wasn't viewed as just a story—it was state policy until 1945.
According to the Kojiki (Japan's oldest chronicle), the gods stood on the heavenly bridge, stirred the ocean with a jeweled spear, and the brine that dripped down formed the islands of Japan. The Japanese people—and specifically the Imperial line—were said to be direct descendants of these gods.
While modern Japan is overwhelmingly secular and atheist, mythologies leave deep cultural scars. Just as secular Westerners still structure their worldviews around concepts of good vs. evil derived from Christianity, secular Japanese culture still carries the artistic and psychological echo that their land is uniquely sacred, deliberate, and central.

3. "Nihonjinron" and the Need for Distinctiveness

In the post-WWII era, as Japan rebuilt itself into an economic superpower, a massive literary and cultural genre emerged called Nihonjinron (literally, "theories about the Japanese people").
Because Japan had become highly westernized, globalized, and technologically advanced, there was a deep national anxiety about losing their identity. To counter this, authors, media, and politicians heavily pushed the idea that the Japanese are genetically, culturally, and linguistically unique from the rest of humanity.
This is exactly the climate that allowed the archaeological hoaxer, Shinichi Fujimura, to thrive. When he claimed humans had been in Japan for 600,000 years, he wasn't just finding old rocks; he was giving a secular, modern audience "scientific proof" of what their old myths used to promise: We didn't just come from somewhere else. We have always been uniquely here.

In Media: The "Center of the Universe" Trope

When creators like Hideaki Anno write sci-fi, they are playing with these exact cultural ingredients. By placing the "Black Moon" and the mother of humanity directly under Japan, Evangelion perfectly bridges the gap between secular sci-fi and ancient Shinto isolationism. It gives the audience that comfortable, deeply ingrained feeling that Japan is the center of the narrative cosmic stage, even if every person watching the show knows the scientific reality of human evolution.

• The Metaphor Shield: Instead of using explicit political propaganda, postwar creators began using sci-fi and fantasy metaphors to scratch that nationalist itch. The Problem in Miyazaki and Evangelion

While Hayao Miyazaki is famously an explicit, vocal leftist and pacifist who frequently criticizes modern right-wing Japanese politicians, his work still heavily relies on a deeply conservative, nostalgic pastoralism. Films like My Neighbor Totoro or Princess Mononoke depict an idealized, pristine, spiritually "pure" ancient Japan. While intended as environmentalist critiques, this intense obsession with a lost, pure Japanese past heavily overlaps with the exact cultural nostalgia that right-wing nationalists use to argue against immigration and global integration today.
In Evangelion, Hideaki Anno actually subverts this, but in a very dark way. He acknowledges the nationalist isolationism—NERV is literally an autocratic, unaccountable military state hiding secrets from the world—but he portrays it as a toxic, miserable psychological prison. The characters are completely isolated, emotionally broken, and unable to connect with anyone else. Anno’s metaphor is essentially showing that the "isolated island fortress" mindset leads to absolute psychological ruin. Yet, because the mecha designs look cool and the lore is deeply mysterious, the broader audience still walks away romanticizing the aesthetic of the fortress anyway.

To clarify the historical parallel regarding global empires: while the US historically preferred economic hegemony and heavy-handed interventionism over direct colonial governance, it did explicitly hold official imperial territories after the Spanish-American War—most notably occupying and ruling the Philippines as an official colony for nearly five decades, alongside Puerto Rico and Guam.

They bury that defensive ethnocentrism in breathtaking metaphors, creating an entertainment industry that allows the domestic audience to feel uniquely special, while foreign audiences applaud it as high art.

The Erasure of Japan's Wartime Children

During the war, the Japanese military operated a vast, institutionalized system of forced military prostitution across occupied territories, most famously affecting women from Korea, China, the Philippines, and the Netherlands (often referred to under the euphemism "comfort women"). In addition to this system, returning Japanese soldiers routinely engaged in unchecked, widespread sexual violence against local populations in occupied zones.
Naturally, this resulted in thousands of pregnancies and children born across Asia, yet these children are completely erased from Japan's national consciousness for two major reasons:
1. They Are Not in Japan: Unlike the mixed-race GI babies who were born inside Japan and visible to the Japanese public, the children of Japanese soldiers were born in Korea, China, the Philippines, Indonesia, and Vietnam. Because they belonged to foreign nationals in countries Japan had colonized, the postwar Japanese government simply washed its hands of them, treating them as an external issue that didn't exist within their borders.
2. The "Kopino" and "Lai Dai Han" Comparison: While the world has slowly started to look at the children left behind by foreign militaries (such as the Lai Dai Han—children born to Vietnamese women and South Korean soldiers during the Vietnam War), Japan has fought fiercely against any legal or historical recognition of its own wartime offspring. To acknowledge these children would mean legally admitting to the systematic sexual violence that created them—something modern Japanese nationalist politicians actively deny ever happened.

The Double Standard of "Purity"

This connects directly to the Nihonjinron myth of ethnic purity you analyzed earlier. Postwar Japan constructed a narrative where the only victims of sexual violation worth talking about were pure Japanese women violated by barbaric foreign GIs.
• The GI Babies: In the late 1940s and 1950s, the Japanese media ran sensationalized, deeply racist stories about the "problem" of mixed-race GI children, framing them as a tragic stain on the nation's purity caused by foreign invaders. They were treated as outcasts, and places like the Sawayama Miki’s Elizabeth Saunders Home had to be privately founded just to protect and orphan these mixed-race children because the state refused to care for them.
• The Colonial Babies: Simultaneously, the children born to Filipino or Chinese mothers from Japanese fathers were completely ignored. In the Philippines, these children (often called Nikkei-jin) faced horrific discrimination from their own neighbors after the war because their fathers were the hated occupiers. Decades later, many have spent their entire lives fighting just to get Japan to recognize their lineage or grant them visas, usually to no avail.
