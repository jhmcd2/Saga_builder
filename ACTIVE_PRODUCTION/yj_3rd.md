Now I think we've found the actual turning point in Ren's story.

And I particularly like that his involvement isn't initially motivated by a desire to expose the Light, fight the Reach, or become a superhero.

Ren gets involved because there are children in danger, and he knows what it's like to be a child nobody particularly cares about.

He doesn't have powers. He doesn't have an affiliation. He isn't supposed to be there. But he has something the others don't: an ability to look at a problem, understand how its pieces fit together, and start making things work.

And then, after he succeeds, the people who consider themselves the heroes decide he isn't really part of the story.

That's a very different kind of origin story.

1. The Cain incident becomes the beginning of the conspiracy
I like your idea of connecting Cain's failed mission to the wider conspiracy.

Let's make the chip important.

Cain wasn't simply on an assassination assignment. He was retrieving a piece of technology or information that somebody wanted kept out of circulation.

Ren was the unexpected variable.

Cain underestimated his target, the encounter went wrong, and Ren survived. More importantly, the chip didn't end up where it was supposed to.

That gives us a chain of events:

2013: Cain's mission

Cain is sent to retrieve a data chip. His encounter with Ren ends disastrously.

2013–2014: The aftermath

The chip is lost, misplaced, or recovered by someone who doesn't understand its significance. Ren goes on with his life.

2014: Sandra retrieves Cassandra

Cassandra returns to Sandra's custody. Ren and Cassandra are separated.

2016 onward: The consequences

The information on the chip eventually becomes relevant to the Reach and the Light's activities.

One thing I'd avoid is making Ren immediately understand the significance of the chip. It can be something he remembers, something he might have kept, or something that resurfaces much later.

That lets us preserve the idea that he was initially just a kid caught in circumstances much larger than himself.

2. The Reach incident: Ren becomes useful, then inconvenient

This fits particularly well with Season 2's established storyline.

The Reach are abducting young people with the metagene, experimenting on them, and working with the Light. The Team eventually discovers the conspiracy and the League becomes involved.

I'd introduce Ren into one of these operations as an unexpected civilian presence.

Here's how I'd structure it.

The initial situation
Ren encounters a group of captured children or teenagers.

The Team is involved, but Ren isn't.

Perhaps he's discovered the facility through an investigation of something connected to the chip, or he gets caught up in an incident while following a completely different lead.

The important thing is that he isn't invited.

The Team sees:

A non-meta teenager.

No formal training or authorization.

No established relationship with their organization.

Someone who is potentially another person they need to protect.

Ren sees:

Captured children.

An alien facility.

A collection of machinery that can potentially be manipulated.

An escape problem.

And he gets to work.

The rescue
I'd give Ren a very specific contribution.

He doesn't defeat the Reach soldiers. He doesn't overpower anyone.

He figures out something the others haven't.

Perhaps the facility's security system, transportation infrastructure, or containment mechanisms have an exploitable weakness. He uses his engineering knowledge to create an opportunity for the prisoners to escape.

The Team provides the muscle and protection when necessary, but Ren makes the escape possible.

This is important: he doesn't replace the heroes. He accomplishes something the heroes couldn't have accomplished without him.

And the children get out.

Mission successful
3. The discovery of the Light
This is where I'd connect the Cain chip.

Ren discovers that the Reach operation isn't an isolated incident.

There's evidence of cooperation between the Reach and human organizations.

Perhaps the information he finds matches something on the chip from Cain's failed mission.

The chip might contain:

Financial transactions.

Personnel records.

References to covert operations.

Names of intermediaries.

Documentation of technology transfers.

It doesn't need to contain the entire Light's organizational structure.

In fact, I'd prefer it didn't.

Ren discovers enough to establish that somebody on Earth has been cooperating with the Reach.

The Team discovers the larger conspiracy through their own investigation.

This preserves the established importance of the League and Team while giving Ren a genuine contribution to the story.

He isn't the person who single-handedly exposes the Light.

He's the person who finds a piece of the puzzle that shouldn't have been found.

4. The injury and abandonment
"Young Justice" Fireworks (TV Episode 2010) - IMDb
 
This is where I think we need to be particularly deliberate.

Ren gets injured during the operation.

Not a little cut or bruised shoulder. Something substantial enough that his ability to continue functioning is compromised.

But he manages to get himself moving.

The mission succeeds. The prisoners are evacuated. The Team gets out.

And Ren discovers that nobody has accounted for him.

There are several ways to handle this:

The Team assumes he's already been evacuated.

His presence was never formally recorded, so nobody knows he's missing.

The extraction becomes chaotic, and the heroes prioritize the rescued children.

Someone knows he's still there but assumes another person is handling his extraction.

I'd lean toward an unfortunate combination of the first three.

That makes the abandonment especially painful without requiring every hero to be deliberately callous.

Ren isn't part of their organization. Nobody has an assigned responsibility to him. Nobody has even established a procedure for accounting for him.

He was useful during the mission.

But nobody made sure he got home.

And that's where the Enterprise-D symbolism starts to come into play.

5. The lecture: the part that really hurts
This is something I'd absolutely include.

Ren eventually makes it back. He's injured, exhausted, and has just survived something that would have killed most ordinary people.

And what does he get?

A lecture.

Not necessarily because the League doesn't appreciate the rescue, but because they're looking at the situation from an entirely different perspective.

Their concerns might include:

He entered a restricted operation without authorization.

He interfered with an active investigation.

He exposed himself to extraordinary danger.

He endangered the operation by acting independently.

He has no training or powers that would justify his involvement.

From their perspective, they're responsible adults dealing with an irresponsible teenager.

From Ren's perspective, he got children out of captivity, uncovered evidence of a conspiracy, and nearly died doing it.

And nobody seems particularly interested in that last part.

The contrast is especially important if the Team members receive praise from their mentors for their successful operation.

They're the young heroes of tomorrow.

Ren is the civilian kid who needs to learn to stay out of trouble.

That's a very uncomfortable parallel with his childhood.

6. The Enterprise-D moment
Ex Astris Scientia - TNG Movie Reviews
 
I'd actually separate this into two emotional beats.

First: the lecture.

Ren realizes that even when he does something worthwhile, the people in authority still see the things wrong with him first.

Second: the ship.

Later, his personal ship is destroyed after one dangerous mission too many.

This time, he is physically stranded, seriously injured, and genuinely alone.

He survives.

But the ship doesn't.

And now the lesson from the earlier operation comes back with considerably more force.

He cannot assume anyone is coming.

He cannot assume people who call themselves heroes will make room for him.

He cannot assume being useful means being valued.

His childhood dream of exploration hasn't disappeared. But the optimistic assumptions attached to it have.

7. The Cain connection: bringing the story full circle
I particularly like the possibility that Ren's involvement with the Light ultimately traces back to the same incident that separated him from Cassandra.

8. One thing I'd like to settle about the conspiracy
I see two possible directions for the chip, and they have different implications.

Option A: The chip contains direct evidence of the Light.

Cain was retrieving something that could expose the organization. Ren's survival and the chip's disappearance created an unexpected loose end.

Option B: The chip contains evidence of a much smaller operation.

Cain was retrieving information about a specific individual, transaction, or covert project. Ren eventually discovers that the same people and organizations are connected to the Reach.

I actually favor Option B.

It makes the conspiracy feel larger than Ren. He isn't carrying the master key to the entire plot. He's following a trail of evidence that gradually reveals something much bigger.

And it means the Light doesn't necessarily know who Ren is at first.

My biggest recommendation: Don't make Ren's defining moment the day he discovers the Light. Make it the day he realizes that he can save people, do something genuinely heroic, and still be treated as though he was never supposed to be there.

That's the loss of innocence.

He doesn't stop wanting to be an explorer.

He stops expecting the world to make room for him.
