This has the bones of a very good political/technical murder mystery, especially because the apparent “missile malfunction” gradually turns into something much uglier: a conspiracy involving the military, a pharmaceutical company, veterans, and eventually a government operation that may itself be based on fabricated intelligence.

The biggest thing I'd do is tighten the conspiracy chain so the reader can follow it without making it feel too convenient.

1. The opioid

I'd use a fictional drug name that sounds like something you could actually see on a pharmaceutical label:

Veycodan — probably my favorite. Sounds plausibly pharmaceutical without being an obvious copy.
Nexodone
Valdorin
Oxyvane
Durexan — though this has an unfortunate real-world association.
Veycora
Relovex

For example:

Waynard Pharmaceuticals' flagship drug, Veycodan, had once been hailed as a breakthrough long-duration opioid. At its peak, nearly one in four American opioid users had taken it. Waynard's critics called it deliberately addictive; Waynard called that "junk science."

I'd actually avoid making the number literally "a quarter of Americans" because that is an enormous proportion and can make the fictional drug seem less believable. A quarter of Americans who had received a prescription opioid is still staggering while sounding much more plausible.

And the fentanyl revelation is a great late-story twist. I'd make it something the company deliberately concealed, rather than simply "nobody noticed." That gives the FDA investigation teeth.

2. The Mayport suspect

The most natural Navy position is probably:

Gunner's Mate (GM2) — Ordnance/Weapons Systems Technician

A 25-year-old sailor could be a Petty Officer Second Class (GM2) or Petty Officer Third Class (GM3) depending on his career progression.

If he's specifically involved in loading missiles aboard the ships, you could have:

Petty Officer Second Class Mateo Alvarez, U.S. Navy, Gunner's Mate

His job would involve weapons handling, maintenance, safety procedures and preparing ordnance for deployment. He doesn't necessarily need to be a missile guidance expert. In fact, that's useful dramatically: he knows enough about the weapons to be suspicious, but not enough to actually have performed the hack.

I'd make him Hispanic-American rather than simply describing him as "Hispanic" in the narrative, unless his ethnicity is specifically relevant.

And NCIS would more plausibly arrest him, rather than "capture" him.

3. The missile hijacking needs one technical adjustment

This is probably the biggest plausibility issue.

Having the missile simply receive a radio signal saying "NEW LAT/LONG" is a little too easy. Modern weapons would have multiple layers of authentication and guidance.

You can preserve your idea by making the attack exploit a legitimate function of the weapon.

The missile receives authenticated mission data immediately after launch. The conspirators aren't "hacking the missile" in the conventional sense. They're spoofing the communications environment at precisely the moment when the missile expects an update.

That creates a much more interesting mystery.

The investigators initially conclude:

The missile wasn't hacked.

Then:

Someone had convinced the missile that the hackers were the launch authority.

And eventually they discover that the attackers exploited a vulnerability introduced during the Orlando penetration-testing program.

That's a much stronger connection between your evidence and your eventual solution.

4. Your conspiracy could have three layers

This is where I think your story becomes especially strong.

Layer 1 — The sailors

Several sailors secretly installed small transmission devices during the previous deployment.

They aren't masterminds. They're angry, ideologically motivated, and convinced they're stopping an immoral operation.

Their devices create the communications pathway.

Layer 2 — The retired missile engineer

A retired engineer understands exactly how the weapons communicate and how to exploit the vulnerability discovered during the Orlando penetration test.

He's the technical brains.

He doesn't necessarily know everything about the larger conspiracy.

Layer 3 — The veteran

He's the ideological center.

He's the one who believes the administration is committing acts of terrorism and that someone has to stop it.

His wife dying from Waynard's opioid gives the conspiracy an intensely personal motivation.

And then you have a fourth layer:

The old Waynard CEO.

This is where I'd be careful.

If the old CEO secretly engineered the entire thing, ask yourself: what does he want?

If his goal is simply revenge, he risks becoming a cartoon villain.

A more interesting motive would be that he wants to destroy Waynard's current leadership while simultaneously destroying the company's remaining competitors or evidence.

Or perhaps he believes the company has become something monstrous and wants to force its collapse—but he has gone so far that he's willing to manipulate patriotic veterans into committing terrorism.

That makes him much more morally complicated.

5. The CEO twist should happen very late

I wouldn't reveal the old CEO until the reader thinks the case is essentially solved.

The investigators arrest the sailor.

Then the test missile is launched.

It happens again.

The sailor is sitting in custody.

That instantly proves he couldn't have done it.

That's an excellent midpoint reversal.

Then:

The investigation isn't about finding the hacker anymore.

It's about determining how many people are involved.

That's when your protagonist starts uncovering the retired engineer, the transmitters aboard the ships, and eventually the veteran.

6. The yacht attack is excellent

Keep this.

But I'd make the investigators initially believe the missile is targeting another Waynard facility.

Instead it veers toward the Bahamas.

The protagonist realizes the coordinates aren't random.

They're the coordinates of the current Waynard CEO's yacht.

That creates a wonderful moment:

"Why would the missile know where his yacht was?"

Nobody answered.

Then someone in the operations room said, "Because somebody put it there."

And now the investigation becomes a race to find the CEO.

Except...

He's not the mastermind.

He's actually missing.

You can have the protagonist eventually discover that the CEO and his family were stranded on an island after the yacht was destroyed/disabled, which allows the "dead CEO" assumption to be overturned.

7. Your protagonist's former CO is potentially your best character

I wouldn't make the former CO secretly evil.

Make him exactly what your protagonist remembers:

an incompetent man who somehow keeps getting promoted.

That's much more frustrating.

He's now Secretary of Defense because he was politically useful, survived scandal after scandal, and mastered the art of delegating responsibility upward when things go well and downward when they don't.

Your protagonist absolutely despises him.

But the important thing is:

the CO isn't the villain.

The protagonist spends a significant portion of the investigation convinced that the bastard must be involved.

He's not.

He's simply incompetent.

That lets you have some delicious tension without resorting to the cliché of "the guy I hate is secretly behind everything."

And it gives the protagonist a genuine character challenge: he has to investigate someone he hates while deliberately refusing to let that hatred influence his conclusions.

8. The ending you've got is particularly strong

The best part isn't actually solving the missile mystery.

It's what happens afterward.

The protagonist proves:

the missiles were deliberately redirected;
the sailors were involved;
the retired engineer was involved;
the veteran was the ideological organizer;
the old Waynard CEO was ultimately responsible for the larger conspiracy;
the current CEO was innocent;
Waynard had secretly contaminated its own opioid supply with fentanyl;
the company's regulatory corruption was real;
and the government's South American intelligence may also be fraudulent.

And yet the military operation continues.

That's a very good ending for a political thriller because solving the murder mystery doesn't fix the world.

In fact, the protagonist discovers something potentially worse:

The people he was investigating really did commit terrorism.

The company really was corrupt.

The government really did have enemies in South America.

And the intelligence being used to justify the next phase of the war was still fake.

That gives you your sequel hook without requiring the protagonist to discover that everything was a conspiracy.

One structural change I'd strongly recommend

I'd make the story's central question evolve:

Act I:
Why are American missiles flying north?

Act II:
Who is controlling the missiles?

Act III:
Why are they targeting Waynard?

Final investigation:
Who benefits from Waynard's destruction?

Final revelation:
The missile conspiracy and the Waynard conspiracy overlap—but they aren't the same conspiracy.

And then the final sting:

The missiles were being used to stop a government operation that was itself based on fabricated intelligence.

Your protagonist solves the murder mystery, but he doesn't get to solve the political crisis.

That feels very appropriate for the kind of thriller you're building.
