Yes. Actually, I think this is a very good premise for a crime thriller, particularly because the mystery isn't simply who kidnapped the girl? It's what on earth actually happened in that car?

And the best part is that the investigator is approaching the case with an entirely reasonable set of assumptions, but almost every assumption is wrong.

You've got the makings of a rather compelling psychological crime mystery here.

What I particularly like
1. The initial mystery is genuinely intriguing
You have several things that don't quite fit together:

A girl has apparently been kidnapped, but she's recovered.

The suspected kidnapper has disappeared.

The girl is unable to communicate verbally, so investigators initially have to rely on written communication.

The suspect's tracks lead towards the Susquehanna River.

There are very few useful physical clues because of the rain and stony shoreline.

The suspect's behaviour doesn't quite match what investigators expect from a conventional kidnapper.

That last point is especially important.

Your investigator is looking at someone who appears to have abducted a girl, assaulted her, and then inexplicably abandoned her with his own phone.

Why?

That's a fantastic question to build a mystery around.

2. The phone is an excellent narrative device
I particularly like the encrypted drive and WhatsApp.

They give your investigators somewhere to look, but they also introduce ambiguity.

For example:

Is he hiding something criminal?

Is he communicating with accomplices?

Is he involved in something more complicated?

Is he simply an exceptionally private person?

The possibility that the man is desperately lonely, rather than a practiced criminal, gives the investigation an interesting emotional dimension.

I'd be careful not to make loneliness itself a suspicious characteristic, though. Instead, let investigators discover that his social isolation was accompanied by a history of emotional abuse from his family.

That makes him more understandable without necessarily making him sympathetic.

3. The reversal is the strongest part
Excellent twist potential
The revelation that this wasn't actually a kidnapping is very effective.

Consider the sequence from the investigator's perspective:

A girl disappears with a man.

The girl is recovered.

The man vanishes.

Evidence suggests he may have entered the river.

The investigation uncovers an increasingly confusing picture of his personal life.

A body recovered in Newport News potentially identifies the missing man.

Investigators finally reconstruct what happened.

And then comes the realization:

There was no kidnapping. There was a disastrous meeting between two people, one of whom had been living under a false identity.

The man believed he had established a relationship with someone. He travelled to meet her, only to discover that the person he'd imagined himself to be building a relationship with wasn't real.

He panicked.

The girl tried to escape. He attacked her. Then, at some point, he came to his senses.

He gave her his phone, took the weapon, and walked away.

The implication is that he understood what he'd done and believed there was no way back.

That's tragic, disturbing, and considerably more interesting than a straightforward kidnapping.

Three things I'd strengthen
1. Why did he give her his phone?
This is probably the single most important detail to develop.

It's an unusual action, and readers will remember it.

There are several possible explanations:

Evidence: He wants her to have access to the conversations that explain what happened.

Communication: He realizes she needs a way to summon help.

Confession: He wants her to have something that explains his actions.

Desperation: He's no longer thinking rationally and simply gives her the one thing he has that might help her.

I particularly like the idea that the phone contains the entire history of their relationship.

The investigators initially treat it as evidence of criminal activity, but it eventually becomes the key to understanding the tragedy.

2. Make the girl's account gradually change the investigation
I'd avoid having her explain everything immediately.

The initial written communication could be frustratingly incomplete.

For example, she might write:

"He wasn't supposed to be like that."

"I knew him."

"He didn't take me."

"He got angry."

"He gave me his phone."

Those statements would be incredibly confusing to investigators who believe they're dealing with a kidnapping.

And once an ASL interpreter becomes available, they can finally establish a much fuller account.

One important consideration: if she is Deaf rather than temporarily unable to speak, writing should be treated as an imperfect communication method, not as a substitute for an interpreter. Her preferred language and communication needs should be established early.

3. Make the Newport News body identification a genuine investigative breakthrough
This is where I'd be particularly careful with geography and timing.

The Susquehanna is a substantial river system, and Newport News is a considerable distance away, near the mouth of the James River and Chesapeake Bay.

A body recovered there isn't an obvious or straightforward continuation of someone entering the Susquehanna.

That doesn't mean you can't use it. In fact, it could be an excellent complication.

But you'll want to establish a credible explanation for how the body travelled there, or introduce a different reason investigators connect the John Doe to their missing man.

I'd research the relevant waterways, currents, and recovery circumstances before committing to that connection.

One additional idea: Make the investigator's assumptions part of the story
I think there's an opportunity to make this more than a whodunit.

Your investigator is trying to understand a man who, according to the available evidence, appears to have committed a terrible crime.

But the more they learn about him, the less the conventional explanation makes sense.

He doesn't appear to have a criminal history. He doesn't appear to have planned an abduction. He doesn't appear to have any obvious motive for taking this particular girl.

Yet the physical evidence says something terrible happened.

The investigator gradually realizes that they've been trying to solve the wrong crime.

That's a lovely central theme:

Sometimes the evidence tells you exactly what happened, but not why it happened.

My biggest recommendation
Don't reveal the truth too early.

And I'd make the final revelation emotionally complicated.

The man isn't secretly innocent. He did assault her. He terrified her. His actions have consequences, regardless of what drove him to that point.

But he also isn't the person the investigators initially imagined.

That's what makes this premise work.

You have a story about mistaken assumptions, loneliness, deception, online relationships, and the terrible consequences of a moment when someone loses control.

I would absolutely develop this further. The central mystery has enough substance to sustain a full-length crime novel, particularly if the investigator's reconstruction of the man's life is as important as the search for him.
