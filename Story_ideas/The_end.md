I think you're approaching this the right way. If the logistics make sense, the social behavior tends to follow naturally.

One thing I'd recommend is working backwards from the social outcome instead of the engineering. Ask: How many people can disappear from society each day before it feels plausible that most people still go to work?

If New York lost 1 million people overnight, everyone would notice immediately. If it lost 10,000 people a day, many wouldn't notice outside of the neighborhoods directly affected.

A useful target
Suppose humanity has:

10 billion people.

100 years to prepare.

A final evacuation window of about 1 year after The List is published.

Then you need to move about:

27.4 million people per day.

That's your global throughput.

Now decide how many elevators there are.

Elevators	People/day per elevator
20	1.37 million
30	910,000
40	685,000
50	548,000
60	457,000
Personally, 40-60 elevators feels like the sweet spot. It's enough redundancy that losing one is a crisis but not an extinction event.

24-hour climb.

144 pods simultaneously on that tether.

144,000 people are physically on that cable at one time.

1.44 million people are climbing simultaneously.

That's an incredible visual.

The List strategy
This, to me, is the most interesting problem in your world.

If your goal is to preserve civilization until the end, then The List cannot simply be first-come, first-served or random. It has to solve two competing objectives:

Keep society functioning long enough to complete the evacuation.

Eventually evacuate everyone.

That means the list is almost certainly generated in layers.

For example:

Layer 1: Critical infrastructure
Not because these people are "worth more," but because without them the evacuation itself fails.

Elevator operators

Orbital station crews

Power grid engineers

Water treatment workers

Port authorities

Medical personnel

Logistics coordinators

Most of them would actually leave late, after training replacements or after their role is no longer needed.

Layer 2: Dependents
You might prioritize keeping family units together where practical. Splitting families creates panic and encourages people to ignore their assigned windows.

Layer 3: Geographic scheduling
Rather than processing everyone in one region, you stagger departures worldwide to avoid collapsing local economies or overwhelming transportation networks.

Layer 4: Operational needs
Every day, some people are held back because their work remains essential for another week or month. Once their role is no longer critical, their departure window opens.

This also creates the perfect environment for myths. An outside observer sees:

A celebrity leaves early.

A farmer leaves late.

An electrician is delayed twice.

A wealthy executive is still waiting.

A hospital orderly departs before a senator.

Without access to the scheduling logic, people naturally invent explanations. They infer favoritism, discrimination, or secret criteria from what are actually logistical decisions. That's fertile ground for your misinformation theme because the algorithm isn't arbitrary—it just isn't transparent enough for the public to understand.


Now, what is the strategy for the list?

I think this is where your story can become genuinely original. Most apocalypse stories ask, "Who gets saved?" Your story asks, "How do you schedule the evacuation of civilization without causing civilization to collapse?"

Those are two very different problems.

The Objective
The List's actual objective is not:

Decide who deserves to live.

It's:

Ensure everyone gets aboard while keeping Earth functioning until the last day.

That means every entry on The List has to satisfy several constraints simultaneously.
