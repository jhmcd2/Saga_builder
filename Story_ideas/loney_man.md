If I were building a working mock-up of an enterprise product with a limited budget, and some of the desired technology either wasn't affordable or wasn't mature enough yet, I'd focus on proving the core value rather than recreating every feature.

I'd typically skip or simplify things like:

Fancy AI features that don't change the core workflow. For example, replace an AI assistant with search, templates, or manual recommendations.

Real-time synchronization everywhere. A manual refresh or periodic sync is often good enough for a prototype.

Complex automation engines. Instead of a full drag-and-drop workflow builder, hard-code a few representative workflows.

Highly customized dashboards. Show a few fixed dashboards with realistic data rather than endless customization.

Advanced permissions. Basic roles (Admin, Manager, User) are usually enough to demonstrate the concept.

Integrations with dozens of third-party systems. Mock the integrations or implement only one or two key examples.

Scalability features. A prototype doesn't need to support millions of users.

Offline support and edge cases. Handle the common path first.

Pixel-perfect UI and animations. A clean, functional interface is sufficient for testing and demos.

What I wouldn't skip are the things that answer the question: "Does this solve the customer's problem?"

That means investing in:

The primary user workflow.

A believable data model.

A responsive, usable interface.

Enough realistic data to make the system feel genuine.

One or two "wow" moments that demonstrate the product's value.

A useful principle is to fake what's expensive and build what's essential. For example:

Use static or simulated data instead of connecting to every backend.

Simulate AI responses if the intelligence isn't the part you're validating.

Replace complex distributed systems with a single database if the architecture isn't what's being tested.

In other words, build the smallest version that proves the idea works. If users love that version, it's much easier to justify investing in the advanced capabilities later.

That's a fascinating design tradeoff, because the answer depends on what the mission is trying to accomplish.

If the mission is expected to last 200–500 years, and you're carrying humanity's first experimental FTL drive, I wouldn't choose either the Hail Mary or the Hermes. I'd choose something much closer to a Galaxy-class starship—not because of Star Trek aesthetics, but because the engineering philosophy fits the mission.

Here's how I'd compare them.

Design	Strengths	Weaknesses	Best Use
Project Hail Mary's Hail Mary	Small, efficient, low mass	Single point of failure, minimal redundancy	One-way emergency science mission
The Martian's Hermes	Excellent interplanetary transport	Not intended for centuries, limited living space	Mars missions lasting months to years
Galaxy-class philosophy	Self-sustaining, redundant, repairable	Massive, expensive, slower to build	Multi-century exploration
For a mission lasting centuries, a ship has to be more than transportation—it has to be a small civilization.

That means including things like:

Agriculture and food production.

Machine shops and manufacturing.

Medical facilities.

Laboratories.

Large stores of raw materials.

Multiple independent power systems.

Living space that supports long-term physical and mental health.

Recreation, education, and culture.

A small crew packed into a compact vehicle for hundreds of years is likely to face significant psychological and operational challenges. A larger habitat with parks, communal areas, and varied environments is much more resilient.

The prototype FTL drive also changes the equation. Experimental systems tend to fail in unexpected ways. A large ship gives you:

Physical separation between the drive and inhabited sections.

Redundant power distribution.

Multiple engineering teams.

Spare parts and fabrication capability.

The option to shut the drive down and continue as a conventional generation ship if necessary.

One thing I would not copy from the Enterprise-D is its scale. At over 600 meters long and carrying thousands of people, much of that volume supports Star Trek's fictional technologies and mission profile. With today's engineering assumptions, I'd probably aim for something in the 150–300 meter range with a rotating habitat for artificial gravity, modular engineering sections, extensive recycling, and onboard manufacturing.

So the design philosophy would be:

Hermes for speed and efficiency.

Hail Mary for scientific focus and simplicity.

Galaxy-class for survivability, redundancy, and self-sufficiency.

For a centuries-long mission carrying humanity's first FTL drive, I'd lean strongly toward the Galaxy-class philosophy. The first FTL expedition should assume that the drive may fail completely. If it does, the crew still needs a ship capable of operating as a conventional deep-space vessel for generations. Designing for graceful failure is arguably more important than maximizing speed on the first attempt.
